## Monad 
Consider the ordinary functions $f: A \rightarrow B$ and $g: B \rightarrow C$. Then, $g \circ f : A \rightarrow C$. 

But in the real world, computations don't always return values so simply. The return values come with circumstances attached. A hashmap lookup returns a *Maybe B*. A network call *Eventually B*. Whatever the circumstance may be - Maybe, Eventually, your pick - we can call the circumstance *M*.

Then function $f$ can look like $f: A \rightarrow M B$.

And the composition is broken, because $g: B \rightarrow C$ expects a plain $B$.

A monad resolves this issue and makes $A \rightarrow M B$ functions composable. 

It requires two things:

(1) A way to take $B$ to $M B$ - a Maybe B, a list [B].
(2) A way to chain - given an $M B$ and a function $B \rightarrow M C$, produce an $M C$. This amounts to flattening e.g. an $M (M C)$ into $M C$.

With these two, functions like $A \rightarrow M B$ and $B \rightarrow M C$ snap together and the plumbing - such as continuing while only stopping if `Maybe` is `Nothing`, happens automatically inside the chaining.

A monad is also a monoid, because it has to possess the property of (1) associativity - so that chaining can be done without having to worry about parantheses or splitting the chained computation into smaller pieces - and (2) the wrapping must be an identity such that $M M B$ can be flattened into $M B$.

## Functor
A *Functor* is translation between categories that preserves structure. Recall, a category is some objects plus composable arrows. A functor F maps one category into another such that the relations are preserved: Every object $A$ gets sent to $F(A)$, every arrow $f: A \rightarrow B$ gets sent to arrow $F(f): F(A) \rightarrow F(B)$.

## Endofunctor
An *Endofunctor* is simply a functor that maps a category onto itself. For example, it can send a `String` to another ttype in its category such as `List String`.

The "endo" part actually matters, because it means you can apply the endofunctor to its own output, e.g. `List (List String)`.

So to summarise, a monad is a monoid in the category of endofunctors. What's the problem?
